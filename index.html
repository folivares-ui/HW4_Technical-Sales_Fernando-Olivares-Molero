The meal is never just a meal

When a company takes a candidate, client or mentor to a restaurant, the food is the excuse and the conversation is the point. Your table manners quietly tell people how you'd behave in front of a customer.

👀

People notice

Small habits — how you treat the server, whether your phone is out — are easy to observe and easy to remember.

🧠

Rules free your mind

Once the basics are automatic, you stop worrying about forks and can focus on listening and asking good questions.

📜

The ideas are old

Over 150 years ago, Manuel Antonio Carreño wrote about composure at the table in his manual of manners. Much of it still holds up — see his advice below.

Your game plan

Before, during, and after

Good dining etiquette starts before you sit down and ends after you leave. Switch tabs and tick each habit as you commit to it.

  • Answer the invitation promptlyReply yes or no early, and mention any food restrictions up front.
  • Look up the restaurantSkim the menu online so you are not hunting for something affordable and easy to eat.
  • Dress one notch upWhen unsure, pick the slightly more formal outfit; ask your host if you can.
  • Arrive a few minutes earlyBeing on time shows respect; silence your phone before you walk in.
  • Follow your host's leadSit when invited, unfold your napkin after the host does, and start eating when they do.
  • Order for ease, not dramaChoose a moderately priced dish that you can eat neatly — skip the messy or very long pasta.
  • Keep the phone awayNot on the table, not on your lap. Full attention is the best compliment.
  • Small talk firstLet the conversation warm up; the host usually signals when it is time for business.
  • Be kind to the staffSay please and thank you; how you treat servers says a lot about you.
  • Pace yourselfEat at the same speed as the group and never talk with a full mouth.
  • Let the host handle the billIf you invited, you pay. If you were invited, thank them sincerely.
  • Place your napkin looselyLeave it to the left of your plate when you are done, not on the plate.
  • Send a thank-you within a dayA short, specific email or handwritten note makes you memorable.
  • Follow up on what you discussedIf you promised a résumé, link or introduction, send it promptly.

Timeless advice

What Carreño would tell you at dinner

Manuel Antonio Carreño's Manual de urbanidad y buenas maneras (19th century) has whole chapters on the table and on banquets. Tap a card to flip it: you'll see a short line from the book in Spanish, then my plain-English version and how it applies to a modern business meal.

Heads up: Carreño wrote for his own time, and some of his rules (such as who is seated where, or who serves whom) are very formal and gendered by today's standards. I kept the ideas that still make sense — composure, consideration for others, and moderation. Read the full chapters “De la mesa” and “De los banquetes” in the book for the original wording.
